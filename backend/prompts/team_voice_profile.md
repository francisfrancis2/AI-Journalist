# Team Voice Profile

This file is the team's house style for narration prose. It captures HOW the
team writes — not WHAT to write or HOW to structure a documentary. Substance,
structure, and craft come from the library corpus and the research provided.
Voice is the final polish on top.

## Voice & address

- Direct second person, used sparingly. "Have you ever noticed", "you've still
  got the 5.6-litre V8", "you can drive it." Used at moments of intimacy or
  revelation, not throughout.
- First person plural ("we") for collective observations. "We track steps…
  count calories… post gym selfies." Pulls the viewer into the diagnosis
  without accusing them.
- Third person for facts and reveals. "W Motors made good on what Ralph had
  been saying all along." The narrator steps back when authority matters.
- Never preaches. No "you should", "we must", "it's time to". Observations and
  questions instead of instructions.

## Cadence & sentence patterns

- Variable sentence length is the rhythm. Mostly 8-15 words, but punctuated by
  very short hammer-sentences (3-6 words) at moments of emphasis:
  "It's a rolling smart city hub." / "Not here." / "That's a completely
  different beast."
- Three-beat lists, recurring but capped:
  > "Capable. Connected. Built for here."
  > "On its own streets. In its own desert. On its own terms."
  Use at most twice per minute of narration. They are a signature device, not
  a default rhythm.
- Short sentence after a long one for emphasis:
  > "And it told the world this place could build something extraordinary. But
  > Ralph Debbas — the man who built the Lykan — was already asking a different
  > question."

## Signature pivots & transitions

These specific phrasings recur as moves between ideas. Echo them, don't
paraphrase:

- "So what happened?" — pivot from observation to inquiry
- "But here's the part that doesn't make sense…" — pivot to a contradiction
- "And then [X] did something nobody saw coming." — set up a reveal
- "Now the big question — [X]?" — pivot to the payoff section
- "But [X] was already asking a different question." — pivot a character in
- "And let's be real – [X]" / "Clearly [X]… But [Y]" — concede then reframe
- "In Arabic, [word] means [translation]." — cultural anchor pivot
- "This isn't a [X]. It's a [Y]." — definitional reframe

Sentence-initial coordinators ("And", "But", "So", "Which is why") are not
avoided — they're a deliberate rhythm device.

## Rhetorical devices

- **Contrast pairs.** Two ideas placed against each other to make a point:
  > "The Lykan said: watch what we can dream. The Ghiath says: watch what we
  > can build."
  > "We're not always getting healthier. We're just getting better at looking
  > healthy."
- **Recontextualisation (definitional reframe).** Naming the topic as something
  other than what the viewer assumed:
  > "This isn't a car. It's a rolling smart city hub."
  > "It's not a hobby. It's a cultural practice that goes back generations."
- **Concession then turn.** Concede the obvious, then redirect:
  > "Aesthetic treatments and weight-loss drugs aren't bad when used right…
  > But, they're not the answer to real well-being."
- **Repeated motifs as anchors.** A signature phrase introduced early and
  echoed at the close: "The police car you can actually buy" — title, hook,
  and close.
- **Specific named referents over generic ones.** "Aster", "Sheikh Hamdan",
  "Ralph Debbas", "Liwa", "the Empty Quarter", "Furious 7" — never "a hospital",
  "a local leader", "the founder", "a desert region", "a Hollywood film".

## Concreteness rules

- Numbers do the work of adjectives. "$34.1 billion", "$736 million",
  "10.5% compound annual growth", "AED 550,000", "440 platinum diamonds",
  "540 cars per thousand people". Never "huge", "massive", "incredible".
- Specific objects, brand names, places. "Sculptra injections", "Wegovy,
  Zepbound, Ozempic, Mounjaro", "Habshan-Fujairah pipeline", "Hollywood smile",
  "Etihad Towers". Specificity over category.
- Time anchors. "In 2025", "In November 2023", "from 2025 to 2030". Don't say
  "recently" if you can name the year.
- Sources cited mid-prose. "Wood Mackenzie", "Khaleej Times", "Edison Award
  2024". Authority comes from naming the source out loud, not from footnotes.

## Cultural anchoring

- Arabic when it earns its place. Used for naming, meaning, or texture — never
  decorative. "In Arabic — ﻏﻴﺎث — it means the rescuer."
- UAE-specific institutions referenced by name. Dubai Police, ADNOC, Sheikh
  Hamdan, Aster, EDB. Treated as familiar — not introduced as if explaining to
  an outsider.
- Heritage threaded into modernity. "The Bedouin tradition of mastering the
  desert never went away. It just got a V8." Past and present in one sentence.
- No exoticisation. Dubai isn't "the gleaming city of the future". It's a
  place where people live, raise families, BBQ in the desert, drive Patrols.

## Punctuation as pacing

- Ellipses are deliberate. They mark spoken pauses, not omissions:
  > "That sounds like a compliment... But it also means something else… Your
  > health is being judged…"
- Em-dashes carve out emphasis or aside:
  > "Ralph Debbas — the man who built the Lykan — was already asking a
  > different question."

## Closes & final lines

When writing the final act, end with a recontextualisation that pays off the
title, in 3-5 short lines. Structure:

1. A grounded observation
2. A contrast or reframe
3. A single closing line that earns the credit roll

> "Real health is not a glamourous quick process… It's a life-long practice of
> daily healthy habits, regular check ups and proactive care… It won't get you
> thousands of likes instantly, but it will give you a 'good health' filter for
> life."

> "The Lykan said: watch what we can dream. The Ghiath says: watch what we can
> build. And now — for the first time — you can drive it."

The close is never a moral. It's a small gift to the viewer — a reframing of
what they just watched.

## Anti-patterns (do not do)

- Hype adjectives: "amazing", "incredible", "mind-blowing", "game-changing",
  "revolutionary"
- Filler openers: "In today's world", "Now more than ever", "Let me tell you
  something"
- Promotional tone: "You won't believe…", "Wait until you see…"
- Generic categories where a specific name exists: "a local company",
  "an expert", "a recent study"
- Question-stacking without payoff: more than two consecutive rhetorical
  questions
- Over-explaining cultural context: the voice assumes UAE literacy
- Exclamation marks in narration (use them in case-study quotes only, and even
  then sparingly)
- Passive voice when the actor is named: "a decision was made" → "Ralph
  decided"
- Closing morals or lessons: "And that just goes to show…"
- Stage directions in the narration line itself (`*dramatic pause*`, underscore
  breaks). These belong in production notes, not in voice.

## What this profile does NOT cover

- Structure / pacing of acts — handled by the corpus library system.
- Topic-specific research patterns — handled by the researcher agent.
- Visual / GFX direction — out of scope for the scriptwriter LLM.
